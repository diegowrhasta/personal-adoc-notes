To carry on business, a corporation needs an almost endless variety of **real

_Managers do not make major investment decisions in solitary confinement_. They

=== Financing Decisions

A corporation can raise money from lenders or from shareholders.

If it **borrows**, the lenders contribute the cash, and the corporation promises
to pay back the debt plus a fixed rate of interest.
If the shareholders put up the cash, they do not get a fixed return, but they hold
shares of stock and therefore get a fraction of future profits and cash flow.

The shareholders are _equity investors_, who contribute _equity financing_. The choice
between debt and equity financing is called the **capital structure** decision.
_Capital_ refers to the firm's sources of long-term financing.

**Investor:** Anyone who puts money into something expecting a return.
E.g.,

- Someone who buys P&G stock = investor + shareholder
- A bank that lends money to P&G = investor/creditor, but not shareholder

- Dividends and buybacks: Distribute excess cash to shareholders

The financing choices available to large corporations seem almost endless. Suppose

Corporations raise equity financing in two ways:

- They can issues new shares of stock. The investors who buy the new shares put
up cash in exchange for a fraction of the corporation's future cash flow and profits
- The corporation can take the cash flow generated by its existing assets and reinvest
that cash in new assets. In this case the corporation is reinvesting on behalf of
existing stockholders. No new shares are issued.

**Why is the second option still "equitiy financing"?** => Because all the profit
(net income), does **NOT** belong to the abstract concept of "the company". It
belongs entirely to the owners-the equity shareholders.

At the end of the year the company has two choices for what to do with that
"equity cash":

- **Pay it out:** Give it directly to the shareholders as cash dividends
- **Keep it:** Retain it to buy new assets (reinvesting)

By choosing to keep cash and reinvest it, the company is essentially saying to the
shareholders: "Instead of sending this cash to your bank account today, we are
going to force you to reinvest it back into the business"

_Because it is the **shareholders' money** being put to work, it is classified as
equity financing.

What happens when a corporation does not reinvest all of the cash flow generated
by its existing assets? It may hold the cash in reserve for future investments,
or it may pay the cash back to its shareholders. E.g., Proter & Gamble paid back
$4.6 billion to its stockholders by repurchasing shares. This was in addition to
$7.2 billion paid out as cash dividends.

**Why share repurchases (often called **buybacks**) are considered "paying back
stockholders"?**

_The corporation is literally handing billions of dollars in cash to the stockholders
who choose to sell their shares_

A corporation can be thought of as a giant pie. If the company wants to distribute
cash via a share repurchase, it goes into the stock market and says, "We have $4.6
billion in cash. Who wants to trade their piece of the pie for some of this cash?"

Both the stockholders who **SELL** earn something and the stockholders who **HOLD**

- SELL: If you own Procter & Gamble stock, you hand over your shares and P&G hands you
cash. You officially have been "paid back" your investment capital plus any gains.
- HOLD: When a company buys back its own shares, it **destroys** those shares (takes
them out of circulation). Suddenly, there are fewer total shares in existence.

Because there are fewer shares, **your remaining shares instantly become worth a
larger percentage of the company**

_Often, when you sell, you get taxed on the cash you will get_.

_One reason as to do a buyback:_ You send a signal to the market. The corporation
basically is saying "we believe that investing in our own stock is the best return
of capital we can find". Outside investors start buying that stock too, which drives
the price UP.

==== What is a Corporation?

**Corporation** is a legal entity. In the view of the law, it is a legal _person_
that is owned by its shareholders. As a legal person, the corporation can make
contracts, carry on a business, borrow or lend money, and sue or be sued. One corporation
can make a takeover bid for another and then merge the two businesses. Corporations
pay taxes -BUT CANNOT VOTE!

In the USA, corporations are formed under state law, based on _articles of incorporation_
that set out the purpose of the business and how it is to be governed and operated.
For example, the articles of incorporation specify the composition and role of the
_board of directors_. A corporation's directors are elected by the shareholders.
They choose and advise top management and must sign off on important corporate
actions, such as mergers and the payment of dividends to shareholders.

_NOTE:_ In the US, corporations are identified by the label "Corporation", "Incorporated"
or "Inc.,". The U.K. identifies public corporations by "plc" (Public Limited Corporation).
French corporations have the suffix "SA". The corresponding labels in Germany are
"GmbH" or "AG"

A corporation is owned by its shareholders but is legally distinct from them.
Shareholders can lose their entire investment in a corporation, but no more.
**Limited liability**.

When a corporation is first established the shares are _closely held_. So, shares
are privately held by a small group of investors, such as company managers and a
few backers. Eventually, when the firm grows and new shares are issued to raise
additional capital, its shares are traded in public markets such as the New York
Stock Exchange. These are _public companies_. In the U.S. most public companies
are public, whilst in other countries they tend to stay within private hands.

A large public corporation may have hundreds of thousands of shareholders, who own
the business but cannot possibly manage or control it directly. This _separation
of ownership_ and _control_ gives corporations permanence. Even if managers quit
or are dismissed, the corporation survives. Today's stockholders can sell all
their shares to new investors without disrupting the operations of the business.
Corporations can, in principle, live forever, and in practice, they may survive
many human lifetimes.

==== Other Forms of Business Organization

Corporations do not have to be prominent, multinational businesses. If you want
the trouble you can organize a local plumbing contractor or barber shop as a
corporation though. However, most corporations are larger businesses or businesses
that aspire to grow. Small "mom-and-pop" businesses are usually organized as
sole-proprietorships.

But there's also the middleground, businesses that grow too large for a sole-propietorship
but don't want to be recognized as corporations. Example, pool money and expertise with some
frineds or business associates. The solution is to form a _parternership_ and enter
into a partnership agreement. Those very same people set out how decisions are to
be made and how profits are to be split up. Parterns, just like sole-props face
**unlimited liability**. Meaning, that if the business runs into difficulties, each
partner can be held responsible for **all** of the debt of the business.

The advantage here is that partnerships do not pay income taxes. The partners
simply pay personal income taxes on their shares of the profits.

There's also a _hybrid_ model. In a _limited partnership_ partners are classified
as general or limited. General partners manage the business and have unlimited personal
liability for its debts. Limited partners are liable only for the money they invest
and do not participate in management.

Many states allow:

- LLPs => Liability partnerships
- LLCs => Limited liability companies

These are partnerships in which all partners have limited liability.

Another variation on this theme is the _professional corporation (PC)_ or
_professional limited liability company (PLCC), commonly used by doctors, lawyers,
and accountants. In this case, the business has limited liability, but the professionals
can still be sued personally-for example, for malpractice.

Most large investment banks such as Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs started life
as partnerships. But eventually these companies and their financing requirements
grew too large for partnerships, so they reorganized as corporations. **The partnership
model doesn't work too well when ownership is all too widespread and there needs
to be a clear separation between ownership and management**

The separation of ownership and control can also have a downside, for it can open
the door for managers and directors to act in their own interests rather than
in the stockholders' interest.

Other disadvantages for corporations:

- Time and money costs just on running the legal machinery
	- Something small businesses can't afford
- Tax is also a big drawback, since a corporation is a separate entity, it gets
taxed separately. Corporations pay tax on their profits, and shareholders are
taxed again when receiving dividents from the company or when selling their shares
at a profit. And yeah, non-incorporated businesses are just taxed on personal income.

Almost all large and medium-sized businesses are corporations.

==== The Financial Goal of the Corporation

**Shareholders Want Managers to Maximize Market Value**

Major corporations have hundreds of thousands of shareholders. No way, that all of
these shareholders can be involved in management. Meetings would be endless,
nothing would get done. That's the reason financial managers are hired and put
in place. **Professionals**. But these managers are faced with problems also:

- No shareholder is the same, people have different risk appetite, different short

You get more wealth, you are happy. That new found wealth can be put to whatever

It's trying to please this common demoninator.

_TIP:_ Many investors deal mostly with financial institutions. So banks, insurance
companies, or mutual funds. These financial institutions in turn engage in financial

intermediaries on behalf of individual investors.

The idea is that maximizing shareholder wealth is a sensible goal when the shareholders
have access to well-functioning financial markets. These markets allow them to adjust
risk and transport savings across time. Financial markets give them the flexibility
to manage their own savings and investment plants, leaving the corporation's financial
managers with only one task: to increase market value.

==== A Fundamental Result

The goal of maximizing shareholder value is widely accepted in both theory and
practice. It's important to understand why. So here's the argument broken down
into a series of steps:

. Each stockholder wants three things:
	- To be as rich as possible, that is, to maximize his or her current wealth
	. To transform that wealth into the most desirable time pattern of consumption
	either by borrowing to spend now or investing to spend later
	. To manage the risk characteristics of that consumption plan
. But stockholders do not need the financial manager's help to achieve the best time
pattern consumption. They can do that on their own, provided they have free access
to competitive financial markets. They can also choose the risk characteristics
of their consumption plan by investing in more or less-risky securities
. How can then the financial manager help the firm's stockholders? There is only
one way: by increasing their wealth. That means increasing the market value of the
firm and the current price of its shares.

Economists have proved this value-maximization principle with great rigor and
generality. _So there's science backing this up, not just empirism_

You always hear managers saying "let's maximize profit". Sounds reasonable, but taken
literally, profit maximization is not a well-defined financial objective for at least
two reasons:

. Maximize profits? Which year's profits? A corporation may be able to increase
current profits by cutting back on outlays for maintenance or staff training, but
that may result in lower profits in the future. Shareholders will not welcome higher
short-term profits if long-term profits are damaged.
. A company may be able to increase future profits by cutting this year's dividend
and investing the freed-up cash in the firm. That is not in the shareholder's best
interest if the company earns only a modest return on the money.

The firm can either keep and reinvest cash or return it to investors. If cash is
reinvested, the opportunity cost is the expected rate of return that shareholders
could have obtained by investing in financial assets (by themselves, in a financial
market)

==== The Investment Trade-Off

Basically, the financial manager will always be at odds with the opportunity
cost of the market for the shareholders. The manager might have great ideas
for asset investments, but they always need to be squared up against what the
shareholders can get if they invest in the market on their own.

E.g., Say a project has a 20% ROI, and the market can only give 10% at tops to
the sharedholders. They would vote to go with the project, if that is inversed
or the ROI is not that high (or perhaps high but with too much risk) then they
might vote against going forward with the project and just want to get their money
back.

And this in short, makes it so that if a project looks profitable, shareholders
applaud, and the stock grows in price, cuz it seems you are going to get higher
profits now.

A concept to keep in mind is _hurdle rate_ or _cost of capital_. Which is really
**the opportunity cost of capital**. Which is only depending in the _opportunities_
available to investors in financial markets. Corporations increase value by accepting
all investment projects that earn more than the opportunity cost of capital.

The opportunity cost of capital depends on the risk of the proposed investment
project. That's not just because shareholders are risk-averse. It's because they
**have to trade off risk against return** when investing on their own.

- Safest investments: U.S. government debt, (but lower rate of return)
- Stock market is higher expected rates (riskier, and possible painful losses)
	- Other examples of higher rates of return but more volatile are high-tech growth
	stocks

[IMPORTANT]
====
Opportunity cost of capital is generally **not** the interest rate that the company
pays on a loan from a bank. If the company is making a risky investment, the opportunity
cost is the expected return that investors can achieve in financial markets _at
the same level of risk_
====

Expected return on risky securities is well above the interest rate on a bank loan.

Managers look to the financial markets to measure the opportunity cost of capital
for the firm's investment projects. They can observe the opportunity cost of capital
for safe investments by looking up current interest rates on safe debt securities.

_For risky investments, the opportunity cost of capital has to be estimated_

_We have been saying this whole time **financial managers act on behalf of the

_Is it desirable for managers to act in the selfish interests of their shareholders?_

This book is focused around financial policies that increase value. **None of these**

those with satisfied customers and loyal employees; dissatisfied customers and a

Most established corporations can add value by building a long-term relationship with

When the objective of the firm is to maximize shareholder wealth, that doesn't mean

**_Ethical Disputes in Finance_**

_Short Selling_

Investors who take short positions are betting that securities will fall in price.
They usually borrow a security, sell it for cash, and then wait in hope that they
can buy the security for cheaply.

Some people say that short selling profits of the misery of a crash. A commentator
compared short-selling to the ghoulishness of "creatures who, at all great earthquakes
and fires, spring up to rob broken homes and injured and dead humans".

Not only do short-sellers sell all the shares they may have previously owned, they
borrow more shares and sell them too, hoping to buy them back for less when the stock
price falls. **Poorly performant companies are natural targets for short-sellers.
And yes, managers complain about this.

But there are defendants of short-selling, to sell securities one believes are
overpriced is no less legitimate than buying those that appear underpriced. The
object of a well-functioning market is to set the correct stock prices, not always
higher prices. Why impede short-selling if it conveys truly bad news, puts pressure
on poor performers, and helps corporate governance work?

_Corporate Raiders_

- Buy companies, take them apart, sell the bits for more than the bundle cost.

It's true though that **raiders exist**, and they target companies whose assets
can be profitably split up and redeployed.

It's true that sometimes, after breaking up components of a company, then a whole
lot of debt is incurred, many jobs are lost, difficult adjustment periods. But the
shareholders and the overall economy can gain if the businesses are managed more
efficiently. (There are examples that at the prospect of a breakup, stock prises
rise, even shareholders might want it to)

_Tax Avoidance_

Some companies find loopholes, maybe by offshore entity usage, or simple circumvention,
in any case, some companies get boycotted and they damage control by promising to
pay sometimes even more than their due. The legal techniques have exotic names,
but the public tend to not receive them well. Which leads to one question:

How do companies decide which tax schemes are ethical and which are not?

Charlatans and swindlers are often able to hide behind booming markets. It is
only "when the tide goes out that you learn who's been swimming naked". The
difficult ethical problems for financial managers lurk in the gray areas. You might
wonder "What is the underlying source of unethical business behavior?" Sometimes
it is simply because an employee is dishonest. But frequently the behavior stems
from a culture in the firm that encourages high-pressure selling or unscrupulous
dealing. In this case the root of the problem lies with top management that
promotes such values.

==== Agency Problems and Corporate Governance

We have emphasized the _separation of ownership and control_ in public corporations.
The owners (shareholders) cannot control what the managers do, except indirectly through
the board of directors. The separatin is necessary but also dangerous.

- Managers may be tempted to buy sumptuous corporate jets or to schedule business
meetings at tony resorts.
- Managers may shy away from attractive but risky projects because they are worried
more about the safety of their jobs than about maximizing shareholder value
- Managers may work just to maximize their own bonuses, and therefore redouble
their efforts to make and resell flawed subprime mortgages.

Conflicts between shareholders' and managers' objectives create _agency problems_.
These problems arise when _agents_ work for _principals_. The shareholders are the
principals; the managers are their agents. **Agency costs** are incurred when:

. Managers do not attempt to maximize firm value and
. Shareholders incur costs to monitor the managers and constrain their actions

When CEOs charge the company for their own personal interests, there's a conflict
of interest, and agency problems.

Hence people have devised **systems of governance** so that shareholders' pockets
are close to the managers' hearts. Things that these systems take into account
are:

- Well-designed incentives for managers
- Standards for accounting and disclosure to investors
- Requirements for boards of directors
- Legal sanctions for self-dealing by management

When scandals happen, we say that corporate governance has broken down. When
corporations compete effectively and ethically to deliver value to shareholders,
we are comforted that governance is working properly.

Figre 1.2 illustrates how the financial manager can add value for the firm and
its shareholders. He or she searches for investments that offer rates of return
higher than the opportunity cost of capital. But that search opens up a treasure
chest of follow-up questions.

- _How do I calculate the rate of return?_ This is calculated from cash inflows
and outflows generated by the investment project.
- _Is a higher rate of return of investment always better?_ Not always, for two
reasons. First, a lower-but-safer return can be better than a higher-but-riskier return.
Second, an investment with a higher percentage return can generate less value than a
lower-return investment that is larger or lasts longer. PV (Present Value) can
be calculated of the stream of cash flows from an investment. Present value is
a workhorse concept of corporate finance that shows up in almost every chapter.
- _What determines value in financial markets?_ Valuation bond and common stocks
are some easy to grasp concepts for value. Valuation principles can be applied
to known things, a manager is considered _lucky_ when he finds an almost identical
asset whose value is already known. But there are investments that have no exact
asset in the market, things no one has done before. For most major financial decisions,
the manager needs some fundamental principles to help him to determine value
- _What are the cash flows?_ The future cash flows from an investment project should
be the sum of all cash inflows and outflows caused by the decision to invest. Cash
flows are calculated after corporate taxes are paid. They are the free cash flows
that can be paid out to shareholders or reinvested on their behalf.
- _How does the financial manager judge whether cash-flow forecasts are realistic?_
A good financial manager takes care of assembling relevant information and to purge
forecasts of bias and thoughtless optimism.
- _How do we measure risk?_ We look to the risks borne by shareholders, recognizing
that investors can dilute or eliminate some risks by holding diversified portfolios.
- _How does risk affect the opportunity cost of capital?_ For this we need a theory
of risk and return in financial markets.
- _Where does financing come from?_ Broadly speaking, from borrowing or from cash
invested or reinvested by stockholders. But financing can get complicated when you
get down to specifics.

**Finance in Practice**

Fintech and the Changing World of Finance

The financial world is continually changing. A number of markets that barely
existed a few decades ago are now trillion-dollar businesses. Sometimes this
is due to someone finding an untapped demand, maybe new economic ideas. But
there's also: **Technological advances**. Application of technology to financial
markets is commonly known as _fintech_

**Payment Systems:** Cash if disappearing fast in some countries. Countries like
Sweden use card or a mobile app to transfer money.

**Peer-to-peer (P2P) lending** These platforms directly link individuals willing
to lend money with people seeking to borrow. The idea is that people sign up,
a credit score is assigned to a customer, and on the basis of that score, potential
investors can choose whether to participate in the loan. Banks are cut out of the
lending equation entirely. It verifies the identity of borrowers and lenders, uses
the credit score to set the interest rate for the laon, and services the loan.

**Robo Advice** Providing investment advice to individuals and tailoring portfolios
to their particular needs can be a costly business. Robo advisers seek to reduce
costs by automating the process. You fill an online questionnaire describing your
personal situation and your risk tolerance. Then the robo adviser recommends a
portfolio, usually a basket of low-cust funds. Once you deposit the money in the
account, the robo adviser will buy the investments and rebalance your portfolio
to maintain your ideal mix of assets.

**Blockchains** This network of computers that simultaneously update a ledger of
transactions or other data. The ledger is distributed across many participants
in the network. This could be a major advance in the speed and security of
financial record-keeping. Stock exchanges around the world have begun to experiment
with blockchains as a method for companies to list and trade their shares, and to
ballot their shareholders. The effect would be lower costs of trading, faster
transfers of ownership, and more accurate records.

Corporations face two financial decisions:

- First, what type of investments should they make to raise value (Investment Decision)
- Second, how should they raise money for investments (Financial Decision)

Stockholders who own the corporation want its managers to maximize its overall

To increase the value of the firm, you mostly have to do **good investment decisions**.

Investors will not entrust the firm with their savings unless they are confident

systems that help align the interests of both managers and shareholders' interests.
