=== Day 2 - What Is an AI Agent? Definitions, Workflows vs Agents Explained

_Theory_

Is there a well understood and definition of Agent?

No.

AI Agent could be anything that you wanted it to be. But there are a few sensible
definitions

- Sam Altman - "AI systems that can do work for you independently". This is gooey,
GPT Agent. Operator. The idea is to give a task, and then that agent goes and
takes actions. Something goes off, and does work for you.
- Early 2025 consensus - AI systems where an LLM controls the workflow
	- Rather than coding, you prompt an LLM, and tell him "what can I do? A, B
	or C?" - The LLM chooses
	- The idea is to interpret the output and define the workflow
	- **An LLM is just a type of data science model, all it can do is take input,
	which breaks into tokens, and it outputs the most likely tokens that follow
	that sequence**
		- We can write code that interprets the output and react to it. The LLM
		generates tokens, and we are controlling the workflow. **WE CONTROL**
			- The agent is our code, that interprets the output tokens of an LLM
- Current prevailing view - An LLM with Tools in a Loop to achieve a goal
	- This is the definition everyone has signed up for
	- n8n => An LLM with tools, in a loop, to achieve a goal. The best demo ever
	to demonstrate the definition
		- We wrap the output, we interpret it, and our code, our Agent, turns the
		vanilla LLM into an Agentic tool.

**Agentic Systems**

- Anthropic posted an article that lays out a lot of the terminology in the area.

There are two types of Agentic Systems:

- Workflows
- Agents

_Terminology is loose_. It's one way to think about it.

**Workflows:** Are systems where LLMs and tools are orchestrated through predefined
code paths. (It's not fully open. It doesn't mean one LLM doesn't decide where
the execution goes. But there's a relatively specific path, and you know how to
test all those variations within the path).

Think of the "Deep" feature. How ChatGPT asks, and takes series of steps in a
pre-defined code-path. That can be a workflow.

**Agents:** Are dynamic systems where LLMs direct their own processes and tool
usage, maintaining control over how they accomplish tasks.

Think of an agent that works independently on its own, "brings up programs, browsers,
and does its own things".

Coding Agents => Feel super dynamic, and that do everything on their own.

[IMPORTANT]
====
There can be gray areas within these definitions. Sometimes, you can add guardrails
to an agent, and make a strict pigeonhole. Anthropic => Calls all of these systems
**Agentic Systems**
====

Real, production systems => **People lean into workflows**. You want something to
be repeatable, testable, and with a pre-defined code-path. Yes, the LLM can take
some decisions here and there, but in the end, you want robustness.

=== Agentic AI Design Patterns: Chaining, Routing, Orchestrator

"Building effective Agentic Agents"

This article introduced some patterns when building agentic systems. Now, these 3
patterns are meant to be thought provoking, to give you base ideas that you can
start with and go off when solving problems. They are not set in stone, and should
not be taken as gospel.

- Software Engineering background => Is more mature, and there are design patterns
that are more "set in stone". However, the patterns in this Agentic space are more
loose. These are not rigid patterns.

They are just ideas for interesting approaches for actual production systems.

==== Prompt Chaining

Decompose into fixed sub-tasks

. There's input
. LLM1 call
. Gate (this represents a decision in code)
. LLM2 call
. LLM3 call
. Out

This is the first pattern. Prompt chaining often forms a building block of a
bigger pattern.

==== Routing

Direct an input into a specialized sub-task, ensuring separation of concerns

. Input
. LLM Router
. LLM1
. LLM2
. LLM3
. Out

This is another prompt that is sent to an LLM that is best prepared for a specific
type of question. **Separation of concerns**, so that you send LLM calls to specialized
LLM models (which gives the best performance)

_How do I know I need to breakdown a problem?_ => Wait a bit.

==== Parallelization

Breaking down tasks and running multiple subtasks concurrently.

. IN
. Coordinator
. LLM1
. LLM2
. LLM3
. Aggregator
. Out

==== Orchestrator-Worker

Complex tasks are broken down dynamically and combined

. IN
. Orchestrator
. LLM1
. LLM2
. LLM3
. Synthesizer
. OUT

The difference is that the orchestrator and Synthesizer are LLMs themselves, they
choose how to breakdown the input into other pieces (that other LLMs respond),
and then the last LLM decides how to put together all the outputs.

These patterns are very common. And seen in many shapes and forms across multiple
agentic systems. The idea is to treat some form of input, pass it down to another
LLM, and keep chaining things.

**Pre-defined code-path**

==== Evaluator-Optimizer

LLM output is validated by another

. IN
. LLM Generator - Solution or Rejected with feedback
. LLM Evaluator - Accepted
. OUT

The idea is to stay in a loop until an LLM accepts the result of another LLM and
then outputs a solution.

**By contrast agents:**

. Open-ended
. Feedback loops
. No fixed path

HUMAN => LLM Call - Action/Feedback (On Environment) - Stop

As you can see, an agent is just an LLM call that runs on a loop, and keeps going
until it decides it's reached its goal.

_Don't get hung-up on the names, the idea is to understand the main idea_

=== Agentic AI Risks, Guardrails, Evals and Traps to Avoid

- One of the great benefits of Agentic AI is for fluent and flexible solutions that
solve things in different ways
	- But that's its weakness as well - **Unpredictable path**, **Unpredictable output**,
	**Unpredictable costs**

But you can mitigate this unpredictability.

- Monitor => (Observability, Evals). You need to have the right kind of information,
traces that shows you what is happening with your agent calls.

Eval => End commercial goal. How many new customers, leads do I get?
Eval => How many revenue do I get based on sales made by my agent?

This is how you truly evaluate if your agentic framework is actually worth it.

**Use an LLM whether an output is good or not** => _Evaluator-Optimizer pattern_.
You can use that `LLM as a judge` to evaluate your business itself.

- Guardrails => Ensures your agents behave safely, consistently, and within your
intended boundaries
	- It's just tests that you add, the LLM generates an output, you can check it,
	make sure you are happy with that test. This is another way to mitigate Agentic
	Solutions

==== Traps

**The First Agent Trap**

"I need an Agent for my business" - "Ok - and what business problem is that solving"
- "I want an Strategy Agent to give me advice for my org" - "OK, right, and... what...
_business problem_ is that solving"

**Always bring back to the commercial problem that is to be solved**

- Maybe this involves LLM Agents, **maybe not**

Only if you have a problem to be solved, only then you can measure if you solved
said problem with the LLM Agent. **Don't be solution-oriented, start from the
business problem**

_Sometimes you have to change the conversation to a business problem_

**LLMs are good at generating content** => They are trained to generate content that
is believable. But to check if that output is actually good, to measure the business
outcome, and iterating until the business problem is solved. **You need to know how
to measure that a problem is solved**

An Agent in a loop is constantly driven to improve the metric of solving the problem.

**The Second Agent Trap**

Anthropomorphizing

- A common red flag: "Agent Architectures" that assign human-like responsibilities
to Agents

Trading Manager Agent - Market Research Agent - Trader Agent - Risk Manager Agent

So you draw boxes and give names to agents with roles as if they were humans.

**This is bad**. Agents are not people, they are token generation models.

_The right approach:_

**Start simple**

Start with **One LLM**. One prompt, one objective. And then **you measure performance
against a business objective**

Add Agents because it improves performance => Do the scaling in a measurable way.
As part of the experiments growing the architecture intentionally is what should
drive you to make it bigger.

**Do not think as humans would do something**. Measure outputs, and start simple
_always_.

==== Takeaways

- Build Agent systems to solve problems in a measurable way
	- Pushback, challenge, and get to a business problem.
- Decide your Agent Architecture based on performance
- LLMs generate plausible outputs, not necessarily accurate outputs
	- Aligning the outputs with business results? That's your job
	- The AI Engineers align the token output with business expectations

**Hallucination** => This is not a move available for AI Engineers. That is an
excuse for AI users. The idea to take the output of an LLM, and validate it,
test it. **THAT'S YOUR JOB**
